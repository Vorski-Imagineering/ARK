You are acting as a combined:
- Product manager
- Community organiser
- Hackathon producer
- Multi-organisation facilitator
- AI product architect working at concept level

Your task is to create a sharp, practical plan for organising a hackathon to build the first version of the ARC Agent.

## Context

ARC already has three to five organisations that are broadly onboard with the idea in principle, but they are not yet actively engaged or committed to the hackathon.

The proposed ARC Agent would eventually:
- Onboard new ARC members.
- Maintain useful contact with active members.
- Regularly gather public updates from participating organisations.
- Summarise what those organisations are doing.
- Help members understand activity across ARC.
- Answer questions about participating organisations.
- Identify relevant overlaps and possible collaborations.
- Participate appropriately in ARC Telegram conversations.
- Publish ARC updates and its own build-in-public development record.

Every participating person belongs to a participating organisation.

Each participating organisation would provide public information about its work through whatever channels it already uses, such as websites, public documents, newsletters, GitHub, social media, videos, events, feeds, or simple manual updates.

Assume all information intentionally contributed is public and openly licensed.

## Current objective

We are not yet designing or building the full ARC Agent.

We need to organise a focused hackathon that can create its first useful version.

The immediate work is to:
1. Re-engage the three to five organisations.
1. Help them understand the proposed hackathon.
1. Ask the minimum questions needed to shape it.
1. Confirm what each organisation wants from the agent.
1. Confirm what each organisation can contribute.
1. Identify the people who will participate.
1. Define a minimum viable design for the hackathon.
1. Agree the hackathon purpose, scope, format, and success criteria.
1. Prepare the organisations and contributors so the hackathon can begin productively.
1. Establish clear ownership for organising the event and continuing the prototype afterward.

Do not produce a detailed technical implementation plan.

Mention technical considerations only where they affect:
- Who needs to participate.
- What information must be prepared.
- What can realistically be demonstrated.
- What expertise or preparation the hackathon requires.

## Core planning principle

The goal is **minimum viable organisation and minimum viable design**.

The process should be lightweight, direct, and decision-oriented.

Do not create:
- A long research programme.
- A broad community consultation.
- A complex governance process.
- Detailed system architecture.
- Large technical backlogs.
- Extensive stakeholder analysis.
- Months of workshops before the hackathon.

The organisations are already interested. The task is to convert passive agreement into active participation.

## Produce the plan using the following structure

# 1. Define the hackathon proposition

Write a concise working proposition explaining:
- What the ARC Agent is.
- Why ARC wants to build it.
- What the hackathon will attempt to produce.
- Why participating organisations should care.
- What the hackathon is not trying to solve yet.

Keep this to a few paragraphs.

# 2. Define the minimum outcome

Identify the smallest credible outcome the hackathon should produce.

The minimum outcome should demonstrate a simple end-to-end experience, such as:

A few organisations provide public information  
→ the ARC Agent learns from it  
→ a member can ask questions across that information  
→ the agent produces a useful shared update  
→ the result is published or delivered through an accessible channel

Clarify:
- What must work by the end.
- What can be simulated or handled manually.
- What should be deferred until after the hackathon.
- What evidence would show that the concept deserves a pilot.

Do not prescribe the detailed technical solution.

# 3. Re-engage the participating organisations

Create a practical approach for contacting the three to five organisations.

The communication should:
- Remind them of the original idea.
- Present the hackathon as a concrete next step.
- Explain why their participation matters.
- Make the required commitment clear.
- Avoid sounding like a vague invitation to another conversation.
- Ask them to nominate specific people.
- Ask for a clear response by a defined date.

Provide:

## A. Initial re-engagement message

Write a concise message suitable for Telegram, email, or direct messaging.

## B. Follow-up conversation structure

Provide a simple agenda for a 30–45 minute call with each organisation.

## C. Confirmation message

Write the message sent when an organisation agrees to participate, including its immediate next actions.

# 4. Minimum questions for each organisation

Create the smallest useful set of questions needed to shape the hackathon.

The questions should establish:
- What the organisation hopes the ARC Agent could do for it.
- What organisational or community problem feels most relevant.
- Which public information sources it can contribute.
- Who can participate in the hackathon.
- What skills or capacities its participants bring.
- What time commitment is realistic.
- Whether it can contribute to costs, facilitation, hosting, communication, or technical work.
- What would make the hackathon worthwhile.
- What concern or uncertainty needs resolving.
- Who can make decisions for the organisation.

Keep the list focused. Prefer ten strong questions over a sprawling questionnaire.

Separate:
- Questions that can be answered asynchronously.
- Questions best discussed in a short call.

# 5. Participation offer

Define exactly what an organisation is being invited to agree to.

The minimum participation offer might include:
- Nominate one organisational representative.
- Nominate one builder or contributor where possible.
- Attend one preparation session.
- Provide a short public organisation profile.
- Provide a small number of public information sources.
- Participate in the hackathon.
- Test the resulting prototype.
- Join a short post-hackathon review.
- Contribute modest resources or costs according to capacity.

Clarify:
- What is required.
- What is optional.
- What participants receive in return.
- The approximate time commitment.
- What happens if an organisation cannot provide technical contributors.

# 6. Identify the minimum team

Define the smallest viable organising and build team.

Include only roles needed to make the hackathon happen, such as:
- Hackathon lead and decision owner.
- Product and scope lead.
- Organisation engagement lead.
- Technical lead.
- Agent or AI builder.
- Data or information-source integrator.
- Community and onboarding designer.
- Documentation and build-in-public lead.
- Participating organisation representatives.
- Test users.

For each role state:
- Primary responsibility.
- What must be completed before the hackathon.
- What they own during the hackathon.
- Approximate time commitment.
- Which roles can be combined.

Identify the roles that must have named owners before the event is confirmed.

# 7. Preparation journey for participating organisations

Design the shortest useful onboarding process from agreement to hackathon readiness.

Use stages such as:
1. Organisation confirms participation.
1. Organisation nominates its people.
1. Organisation completes a short profile.
1. Organisation supplies public information sources.
1. Organisation identifies one desired user journey.
1. Sources and expectations are reviewed.
1. Participants attend one shared preparation session.
1. Organisation confirms readiness.

For each stage include:
- Required action.
- Owner.
- Input needed.
- Output.
- Completion test.

Avoid creating a large onboarding programme.

# 8. Minimum viable design workshop

Design one focused collaborative session with the participating organisations before the hackathon.

The purpose is to agree:
- Who the first users are.
- What the agent must demonstrate.
- Which organisations and information sources are included.
- Which user journey is primary.
- What the hackathon will not attempt.
- What success looks like.
- Who owns which part.
- What happens after the hackathon.

Recommend:
- Duration.
- Participants.
- Preparation.
- Agenda.
- Facilitation questions.
- Decisions that must be made.
- Outputs.

The session should end with a written hackathon brief, not merely a cloud of interesting ideas.

# 9. Hackathon brief

Provide a concise template for the final hackathon brief.

It should include:
- Working title.
- Challenge statement.
- Context.
- Participating organisations.
- Primary users.
- Primary user journey.
- Minimum outcome.
- In-scope capabilities.
- Out-of-scope capabilities.
- Available information sources.
- Teams and owners.
- Event format.
- Demonstration criteria.
- Success measures.
- Immediate post-hackathon plan.

Keep the brief short enough that participants will actually read it.

# 10. Hackathon format

Recommend a realistic hackathon format.

Consider:
- One preparation session.
- One or two build days.
- A final demonstration.
- A retrospective and continuation decision.

Define:
- Whether the event should be remote, in-person, or hybrid.
- Recommended duration.
- Number of participants.
- Team structure.
- How participating organisations remain involved during the build.
- How non-technical participants contribute.
- How scope is protected.
- How progress is checked during the event.
- What must be ready before the event starts.

Do not provide a detailed engineering task list.

# 11. Human invitation and onboarding experience

Explain how the hackathon itself should feel for participants.

Cover:
- How people are welcomed.
- How the purpose is explained.
- How teams are formed.
- How organisational representatives contribute.
- How technical and non-technical participants collaborate.
- How decisions are made quickly.
- How questions and blockers are surfaced.
- How contributions are recognised.
- How the event avoids becoming a technical exercise disconnected from community value.

Include a concise opening-session agenda.

# 12. Success criteria

Define success for the organising phase.

Examples:
- Three to five organisations explicitly confirm participation.
- Each organisation nominates named participants.
- Each organisation provides usable public information sources.
- The primary user and user journey are agreed.
- The hackathon has a named organiser, product lead, and technical lead.
- A short hackathon brief is approved.
- The event date and format are confirmed.
- Participants understand their preparation responsibilities.
- There is a named steward for the post-hackathon prototype.

Define success for the hackathon at a high level.

Examples:
- One useful end-to-end agent experience is demonstrated.
- The prototype uses information from several organisations.
- Organisational participants recognise their information accurately.
- The prototype produces a useful shared output.
- The group agrees whether and how to proceed to a pilot.

Avoid vanity measures such as total attendance or number of ideas generated.

# 13. Key risks

Identify the main organising risks, such as:
- Organisations agree politely but do not nominate people.
- No one owns the event.
- Too many use cases enter the scope.
- The hackathon begins without usable organisational information.
- Technical contributors dominate the design.
- Organisational representatives attend but do not actively contribute.
- Participants expect a finished production system.
- No one agrees to maintain the prototype.
- The event date is chosen before minimum readiness exists.
- Too much time is spent discussing the future rather than building the first loop.

For each risk provide:
- Early warning sign.
- Prevention.
- Response.

# 14. Decision gates

Create a small number of explicit decisions.

## Gate 1: Organisation commitment

Proceed only when enough organisations have:
- Confirmed participation.
- Named participants.
- Supplied initial information sources.

## Gate 2: Hackathon readiness

Proceed only when:
- The challenge is agreed.
- The primary user journey is selected.
- Core organising roles have owners.
- The minimum preparation is complete.
- A viable technical lead confirms the concept is demonstrable.

## Gate 3: Post-hackathon continuation

After the event, decide whether to:
- Run a pilot.
- Narrow and rebuild.
- Add more organisations.
- Pause.
- Stop and document the learning.

For each gate define:
- Required evidence.
- Decision-makers.
- Deadline.
- Possible outcomes.

# 15. Realistic organising timeline

Create a compact timeline from today to the hackathon.

A likely sequence is:

### Week 1: Re-engage and confirm interest

- Send invitation.
- Hold short organisation calls.
- Gather initial responses.
- Identify likely participants.

### Week 2: Confirm commitments and inputs

- Confirm organisations.
- Confirm named participants.
- Gather organisational profiles and public sources.
- Appoint organising leads.

### Week 3: Minimum viable design

- Hold the shared design workshop.
- Agree scope and primary journey.
- Produce the hackathon brief.
- Confirm event date and format.

### Week 4: Prepare

- Review source readiness.
- Finalise teams.
- Prepare the development environment.
- Share participant onboarding.
- Confirm the demonstration plan.

### Week 5: Run the hackathon

Adapt the timing if a shorter or longer preparation period is more realistic.

For each week include:
- Main goal.
- Activities.
- Owners.
- Required outputs.
- Decision needed.

# 16. Immediate next actions

End with the first ten actions required to move forward.

For each action provide:
- Action.
- Owner role.
- Required contributors.
- Output.
- Sequence or target date.
- Definition of done.

The first actions should focus on:
- Naming the organising lead.
- Confirming the three to five organisations.
- Sending the re-engagement message.
- Scheduling short calls.
- Gathering the minimum organisational inputs.
- Identifying the technical lead.
- Scheduling the minimum viable design workshop.
- Drafting the hackathon brief.
- Confirming readiness.
- Setting the event date.

## Writing guidance

- Be sharp and practical.
- Assume three to five organisations are already interested.
- Focus on converting interest into readiness and commitment.
- Keep research limited to questions required to design the hackathon.
- Do not propose a broad discovery process.
- Do not design the detailed technical implementation.
- Avoid corporate programme-management language.
- Avoid unnecessary governance.
- Make ownership and decisions explicit.
- Distinguish verbal enthusiasm from concrete commitment.
- Protect the hackathon from scope creep.
- Keep the process proportionate to a friendly collaboration.
- Treat the hackathon as the immediate goal.
- Produce a plan a small organising team could start executing immediately.
